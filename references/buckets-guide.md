## Two Sides of Content Territory

Discovering what to write about has two complementary dimensions:

**Creator side — Genius Zones:** What are you uniquely positioned to write about? This comes from the intersection of your expert zones — the combination of skills, experiences, and knowledge that gives you an "unfair advantage" over other writers in your space.

**Audience side — Content Buckets:** How do you slice your expertise for different readers? Content buckets are audience-level categories that help you balance reach (broad, universal content) with depth (niche, expert content).

The genius zone tells you *what territory to claim*. The content buckets tell you *how to serve different audiences* within that territory.

## The Four Zones (Hendricks / Steimle)

From _The Big Leap_ by Gay Hendricks, adapted by Josh Steimle:

1. **Zone of Incompetence** — Things you're simply not good at. Avoid writing about these.
2. **Zone of Competence** — You can get it done, but no better than the next person. Not where your writing voice will shine.
3. **Zone of Excellence (Expert Zone)** — Things you're very, very good at compared to most people. This is where most people get stuck — you *could* write about it, but it may not be your calling.
4. **Zone of Genius** — Where natural talent meets genuine enjoyment. You have what could be called an "unfair advantage." This is where your most compelling, sustainable writing comes from.

The key insight: your problem isn't that you lack expertise — it's that you're good at too many things. The genius zone is the antidote to this paralysis.

## Finding Expert Zones

Expert zones are everything you know enough about to help someone who knows nothing. They include:

- Professional skills and experience (marketing, engineering, sales, teaching)
- Life experiences (living abroad, parenting, career changes, overcoming challenges)
- Industries you've worked in (SaaS, healthcare, finance, education)
- Hobbies and interests (skateboarding, trail running, cooking, gaming)
- Demographics and perspectives (age, culture, generation, geography)
- Academic knowledge (degrees, certifications, self-study)

You don't have to be THE BEST at something to be an expert. If you're a beginner at trail running, you're still an expert at what it's like to be a beginner trail runner — and that's valuable to someone starting out. "If you don't know anything yourself, you can become an expert by researching the topic and staying one step ahead of your audience, or interviewing other experts." (Steimle)

## Finding the Genius Zone

The genius zone lives at the **intersection** of expert zones. The method:

1. List all your expert zones (aim for 10–20)
2. Identify your **One Big Key Zone** — the one that has to be included. Usually the area with the most experience or deepest knowledge.
3. Identify **secondary key zones** — 2–4 zones that, when combined with the big one, create something unique.
4. The overlap of these zones is your genius zone.

Example: "Marketing" alone isn't unique — millions of people know marketing. "Marketing × Skateboarding" puts you in a very small group. "Marketing × China × Entrepreneurship × Thought Leadership" makes you essentially one of a kind.

Your genius zone can combine two areas, or three, or ten. It might be language + country + degree + work experience + hobby.

> "What position do you want to occupy in your audience's mind?" — Al Ries and Jack Trout, _Positioning_

## Content Buckets

Once you know your genius zone, you translate it into three content buckets (p. 263):

### 1. General Audience
Universal topics anyone can relate to — life lessons, productivity, mindset, personal growth. These have the broadest reach and highest viral potential. They're the entry point for readers who don't yet know they need your niche expertise.

### 2. Niche Audience
Hyper-relevant to your expertise and genius zone. The more specific, the better — refine "Sales" into "Cold Email Outreach For Sales" (p. 284). This is where you establish credibility and become the go-to voice.

### 3. Company/Industry Audience
Relevant to a specific industry or professional community. Positions you within a context — the person who understands this particular world and speaks its language.

The first six months should be spent exploring, practicing, and gathering data across all three buckets (p. 263). Use engagement data to discover which bucket and which specific topics resonate most.

---

## From Buckets to Topics

Each content bucket should have 3+ recurring topics underneath it. These topics are the themes you return to repeatedly — because repetition is how people remember you (p. 281). The same "story" gets told hundreds of different ways.

**Example mapping:**

Genius Zone: Marketing × China × Entrepreneurship

| Bucket | Topics |
|--------|--------|
| General Audience | Decision-making under uncertainty, learning from failure, cross-cultural communication |
| Niche Audience | Market entry strategies for China, marketing in non-English-speaking markets, building brands across cultures |
| Industry Audience | SaaS expansion into Asian markets, MarTech for global companies, lessons from Western brands in China |

## Connecting Buckets to Category Creation

Your genius zone and content buckets connect directly to category creation (p. 79). Three combination methods for creating a unique category:

- **Audience × Genre** — e.g. "productivity for game developers" (p. 82)
- **Genre × Genre** — e.g. "stoicism meets startup culture" (p. 82)
- **Audience/Genre × Tone** — e.g. "finance explained with humor" (p. 83)

Your genius zone *is* your category. Your content buckets are how you serve different segments within that category.

## The Specificity Principle

The broader you are, the more confusing you are (p. 64). You want people to know where to put you on the bookshelf in their mind (p. 66). Start with a niche, then expand (p. 68).

This applies at every level:
- Your genius zone should be specific enough that few people occupy the same intersection
- Your niche bucket should be specific enough that readers think "this is exactly for me"
- Your topics should be specific enough to pass the test: "Can I make this more specific?"

---

## Buckets Evolve With Data

Content buckets are a starting point, not a permanent commitment. As you write and gather data:

- Pay attention to which topics attract more viewership (p. 46)
- Start gathering data about your most popular categories (p. 123)
- Data doesn't lie, but it reflects the external crowd, not necessarily your internal compass (p. 124)
- Optimize for what performs so you can introduce people to what you care about (p. 125)
- Refine buckets based on what resonates — narrow "Sales" to "Cold Email Outreach For Sales" when the data points that way (p. 284)

Purpose anchors you. Genius zones define your territory. Content buckets organize your output. Data refines all three over time.
