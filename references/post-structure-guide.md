## Guiding Principles

These principles apply to every section of every post. They're not just theory — they're the lens through which to evaluate every structural choice and every revision pass.

**Rate of Revelation** (p. 63): The speed at which you reveal new, important information to the reader. Every single sentence should advance the "story" to the next point. The higher the rate, the more the piece "wins." This is one of the most important qualities to optimise for, but not the only one — specificity, credibility, and emotional resonance matter too.

**The Golden Rule** (p. 242): You are not the main character in your story. The reader is. The Golden Intersection of great writing: **Answering the Reader's Question × Telling Them an Entertaining Story** (p. 244). At no point should your own story or context exceed the space you spend talking about the reader (p. 253). Only mention information truly relevant to the topic at hand (p. 254). Make whatever you're promoting "the setting" for the story, not the story itself (p. 255).

**Specificity** (p. 64–68): The broader you are, the more confusing you are. You want people to know where to put you on the bookshelf in their mind. The more specific you are, the more clarity you gain as a writer — and the reader feels like they've found exactly who they were looking for.

## Process — Before You Write

**Skeleton first** (p. 213): List out your main points before you begin writing. This forces clarity about what you're actually arguing and reveals the natural shape of the piece — how many points you have, how deep each one needs to go, and what structure will serve them best.

**Know your writing type.** From Chapter 6 (p. 150–163), there are five types. Most pieces lean toward one, but they can blend:

- **Actionable Guide** — Comprehensive, structured how-to content. Needs room for explanation and steps.
- **Opinion** — A clear stance backed by quotes, stories, and clarity. Needs a strong opening position.
- **Curated List** — A collection of examples or resources. Speed and specificity are everything.
- **Story** — A narrative built around conflict or achievement. Needs an emotional hook.
- **Credible Talking Head** — Authority-driven writing. Needs context for why your perspective is different.

**Decide how many Main Points you need.** The writing type and the skeleton will tell you. The more Main Points you have, the less explanation you can afford for each one (p. 204). A piece with 2–3 points has room for depth, stories, and build-up. A piece with 5+ points needs to move fast — every sentence must earn its place. (Target word count is set by the calling skill, not by this guide.)

## The Headline

Write the headline before you write the post. It's a micro version of your entire piece (p. 169) and it sets the promise your introduction must deliver on. If the headline and introduction don't align, the reader feels cheated — and you've lost them (p. 149).

A headline must communicate three things (p. 164):

- **What** this piece is about
- **Who** it's for
- **The Promise** — the problem solved or solution offered

### The Curiosity Gap

Tell the reader what it's about, who it's for, and what it's promising — all without revealing the answer (p. 165). Number-style headlines ("7 Things," "3 Mistakes") are not clickbait as long as you keep your promise (p. 166). They're "powerful because they are clear in their intentions" (p. 167).

### Anatomy of a Headline

Using the template (p. 174): **The 1** ==Question== \*That Gets\* *Every Single Millennial* {In Trouble}

- **The 1** — A number conveys conviction. It is declarative and implies a strong stance. And it sounds short.
- ==Question== — The topic word: Reason, Way, Solution, Problem, etc.
- \*That Gets\* — The action verb. Pick strong, descriptive, conversational words (gets, incentivizes, pushes, encourages). "Gets" is conversational (p. 176).
- *Every Single Millennial* — The who. Define your audience by age, interest, location, niche — then narrow further. The "Who within that Who."
- {In Trouble} — The promise. What outcome or tension does the reader walk away with?

### Proven Headline Formats (p. 178–181)

Big Numbers, Dollar Signs, Credible Names, "This Just Happened," The Success Story, Things That Shouldn't Go Together, For The Industry, The Topic Within The Topic (add a second headline in parentheses), Question/Answer, X Number. These formats can be combined and aren't tied to a single writing type — pick based on which qualities (authority, urgency, curiosity, specificity) your piece needs most in its headline.

### Headline Rules

- Declare the content, the promise, and the audience; eliminate all "tiny words" (p. 182)
- Add power phrases: crucial, unforgettable, eye-opening, painful, emerging — these hint at a more meaningful or impactful promise (p. 184)
- Write 30 versions of every headline before picking one (Buzzfeed rule, p. 181)

## The Three Essential Sections

Every post follows the same skeleton (p. 186): an Introduction, Main Points, and a Conclusion. The frameworks below are not exclusive choices — you can mix them within a single piece, and many posts combine elements from several. The descriptions below highlight what each framework *favours*, so you can judge which qualities your piece needs most at any given moment.

Remember: the headline sets the promise. The introduction must deliver on it. The main points must fulfil it. The conclusion must close it. Every section is in service of the same contract with the reader.

---

## Part 1 — Introduction Frameworks

The introduction must answer three questions instantly (p. 186): What is this about? Is this for me? What are you promising — and how confident am I that you'll deliver? When the first question is answered, you have a very limited amount of time to answer the second and third.

### 1/3/1 (p. 188) — favours clarity and speed

The most versatile shape. "Easy to read" (p. 190). The constraint of three middle sentences forces you to be concise, which means the reader gets to your promise quickly. A good default when you don't have a strong reason to use something else.

> **The first sentence is your opener.**
>
> The second sentence clarifies your opener. The third sentence reinforces the point you're making with some sort of **credibility or amplified description**. And the fourth sentence rounds out your argument, guiding the reader toward your conclusion.
>
> **The fifth sentence is your strong conclusion.**

The last sentence should be **both a conclusion and a transition into the next section** — your main points (p. 189).

### 1/5/1 (p. 190) — favours context and nuance

Same shape as 1/3/1 but "with more room for description and clarification." The extra sentences let you build on credibility, give added context, or explain *why* you're telling the reader this. Reach for this when the topic genuinely needs more setup before your promise will land — but be careful: every extra sentence slows down the Rate of Revelation. If those extra two sentences aren't earning their place, a 1/3/1 will hit harder.

> **The first sentence is your opener.**
>
> The second sentence clarifies your opener. The third sentence reinforces the point you're making with some sort of credibility or amplified description. The fourth sentence builds on that credibility or description, giving added context or new information. The fifth sentence explains to the reader why you're telling them what you're telling them. And the sixth sentence drives home the point.
>
> **The seventh sentence is your strong conclusion.**

### 1/3/2/1 and 1/5/2/1 (p. 192) — favours conviction and justification

Adds a two-sentence conclusion block: a strong statement plus an expansion of *why* you're making such a strong conclusion, before the transition sentence. The double conclusion gives you space to not just state your position but defend it. Useful when the promise itself might surprise or challenge the reader and needs a beat of "here's why I'm saying this" before moving on.

> This first sentence is your opener.
>
> This second sentence clarifies your opener. This third sentence reinforces the point you're making with some sort of credibility or amplified description. And this fourth sentence rounds out your argument, guiding the reader toward your conclusion.
>
> This fifth sentence is your strong conclusion. And this sixth sentence is expanding on why you're making such a strong conclusion.
>
> This seventh sentence is what you're going to talk about next.

### 1/4/1/1 (p. 195) — favours emotional resonance

The fourth body sentence explicitly speaks to the emotional benefit of the reader. Then a conclusion, followed by a sentence on why that conclusion matters. This makes the reader feel the piece is for *them*, not just about a topic. Particularly strong when the payoff is personal — growth, relief, transformation.

> The first sentence is your opener.
>
> This second sentence clarifies your opener. This third sentence reinforces the point you're making with some sort of credibility or amplified description. This fourth sentence rounds out your argument. And this fifth sentence speaks to the emotional benefit of the reader.
>
> This sixth sentence is your conclusion.
>
> And this seventh sentence is why that conclusion matters so much.

### 1/3/1 + 1/3/1 (p. 195) — favours contrast and reframing

Two stacked 1/3/1 blocks. The first sets up one angle; the second reframes or deepens it. The final sentence is the combined conclusion. The double structure lets you set up a tension — here's what most people think, now here's the twist — before diving into the body. It takes more space, so it trades some speed for richer setup.

> **The first sentence is your opener.**
>
> The second sentence clarifies your opener. The third sentence reinforces the point you're making with some sort of **credibility or amplified description**. And the fourth sentence rounds out your argument, guiding the reader toward your conclusion.
>
> **This fifth sentence is your conclusion.**
>
> Now, here's a new first sentence as a second opener.
>
> And this second sentence clarifies your second opener. This third sentence reinforces the new point you're making — with some sort of credibility or amplified description. And this fourth sentence rounds out the second point of your argument.
>
> This fifth sentence is the big conclusion of your introduction.

### 1/3/1 + Bullets (p. 199) — favours preview and scannability

The conclusion of the 1/3/1 block doubles as the subhead for a bulleted list of main points. The reader gets an instant map of what's coming, which maximises Rate of Revelation in the intro itself. Particularly natural when the piece has clearly distinct points the reader can evaluate at a glance.

> **The first sentence is your opener.**
>
> The second sentence clarifies your opener. The third sentence reinforces the point you're making with some sort of **credibility or amplified description**. And the fourth sentence rounds out your argument.
>
> **This fifth sentence is your conclusion, as well as the subhead of your bulleted list.**
>
> - Point #1
> - Point #2
> - etc.

### 1 + Subhead (p. 200) — favours maximum speed

A single sentence, then straight into the first subhead. Skips preamble entirely, trusting the headline and the first sentence to do all the setup work. Strong when the headline already did the heavy lifting, or when the audience already knows and trusts you.

> This first sentence is what the whole piece is about.
>
> **And this first subhead is what we're going to dig into right here, right now.**

## Part 2 — Main Point Frameworks

These frameworks shape how you develop each individual Main Point. A single piece can use different frameworks for different points — a deep 1/2/5/3/1 for your most important argument and a fast 1/3/1 for a supporting one, for example. Think about rhythm across the whole piece: if every Main Point uses the same framework, the writing can feel monotone. Varying the depth and pace between points keeps the reader engaged.

### 1/2/5/3/1 (p. 205) — favours depth and persuasion

"A good framework for making a solid argument without getting lost in the sauce" (p. 205). The five-sentence expansion block gives room for a story or credible insight, which builds trust. The trade-off is length — a point structured this way uses significant word budget, so it works best when you have few points and want each one to land with weight.

> This first sentence is your opener.
>
> The second sentence clarifies your opener. And this third sentence is why the reader should care.
>
> This fourth sentence starts to expand on the point. This fifth sentence is a story, or some sort of credible piece of insight. This sixth sentence builds on that story or insight and tells the reader about something they maybe didn't know. This seventh sentence is a small conclusion. And this eighth sentence is why that conclusion matters.
>
> This ninth sentence recaps what you just told the reader. This tenth sentence reinforces the argument you're making with an additional tidbit or insight. And this eleventh sentence drives the point home.
>
> This twelfth sentence reminds the reader of the important takeaway.

### 1/3/1 (p. 208) — favours speed and directness

"Speed is how you win on the internet" (p. 208). Works well when you have more than three Main Points and need each one to be punchy rather than deep. The key move: "Skip right to the end. Tell the reader why this one specific Main Point matters to them" (p. 209).

> This first sentence is your opener.
>
> The second sentence clarifies your opener. And this third sentence is why the reader should care. This fourth sentence is your mini conclusion.
>
> This fifth sentence is why that mini conclusion matters specifically to your target reader.

### 1/1/1+ (p. 211) — favours dramatic pacing and emphasis

Every sentence stands as its own paragraph. Each statement gets visual weight and a pause. Works for "individual ideas, statements, or descriptions" (p. 211). But it's "easy to abuse" — if used for too long, writing ends up feeling "over-caffeinated and monotone" (p. 211). Best used as a momentary gear shift within a piece, not as the dominant structure throughout.

### Main Point / 1, 2, or 3 (p. 213) — favours extreme brevity and scannability

A subhead plus one to three sentences max. Forces you to ask: "How can I make those two sentences as impactful as possible?" (p. 213). Works especially well when each subhead is a sentence or thought in itself, so that skimming the subheads alone conveys the gist of the entire piece (p. 156). The trade-off is that there's no room for stories, build-up, or nuance — the subhead has to do most of the work.

> **You have your main point here.**
>
> And then you have one, two, or three sentences (max) expanding on that Main Point.

### Formatting Techniques

These techniques work across all frameworks and can be layered on top of any structure.

**Short / Long / Long / Short** (p. 220): Alternate paragraph lengths. Never have three or more long paragraphs in a row. This rhythm keeps the eye moving and prevents the reader from feeling overwhelmed by dense blocks.

**Bolded Statements** (p. 220): Bold sentences that function as subheads within prose. Good when the piece is too narrative for formal subheads but still needs scannability — the reader's eye can jump from bold to bold to get the shape of the argument.

**Bold the first half of a sentence** (p. 227): Signals to the reader what each paragraph covers at a glance. "I chose to separate each 'chapter' and then bold the first half of the sentence to signal to the reader, 'In this paragraph we're talking about gaming. In this paragraph, we're talking about bodybuilding...'" The key is to tie the bolded portion to one paragraph and one paragraph only (p. 227).

**Repetition** (p. 221): Repeat a phrase or structure across Main Points for rhythm and memorability — the "I have a dream" approach. Creates a sense of building momentum. Particularly strong in opinion-driven or emotionally charged writing.

**First, Second, etc.** (p. 222): Turn what could be a bullet list into full narrative sentences. A form of repetition that adds flow. Useful when you want ordinal structure but a numbered list feels too dry or disconnected from the prose.

## Part 3 — Conclusion Frameworks

Conclusions are optional in online writing. When you do write one, it's descending action — "more about reflection and meaningful takeaways" (p. 229). The choice depends on what you want the reader to feel or do after finishing.

### The Cliff (p. 230) — favours continued engagement

End with forward momentum — make the reader want to read more. No neat bow. Strong when the piece is part of a series or when you're building an audience and want the reader to click through to more of your work.

### The Extended Final Main Point (p. 223) — favours natural flow

Just extend your final thoughts with an extra paragraph or sentence. Avoids the artificial feeling of a formal conclusion when the piece already feels complete. Works well when your last Main Point is your strongest and a separate wrap-up would dilute it.

### The Summary (p. 236) — favours actionability

Checklists, action steps, quick recaps, small reminders. "Readers love" these (p. 236). Particularly natural after a piece that taught something concrete — the reader walks away with a takeaway they can use immediately.

### Strong Opinion (p. 238) — favours conviction and directness

Just say what you want to say. "You want them clicking to read more and more and more of your stuff — without them feeling tricked or dragged along" (p. 238). Works when the piece built an argument and needs a confident, declarative close rather than a summary or a cliffhanger.

## Talking About Yourself (p. 242–257)

Personal stories and self-promotion are not forbidden — but they must serve the reader.

- Talking about yourself is valuable **for the purpose of giving important context** to the reader (p. 243). There's a difference between talking about yourself because you want to and talking about yourself because it helps the reader understand.
- The Golden Intersection puts the reader first: this piece is about their questions, their wants, their desires (p. 247).
- At no point should your story or "for context" information equal or exceed the amount of time you spend talking about the reader (p. 253).
- Never dedicate an entire section, no matter how big or small, to your own self-promotion (p. 254).
- Make whatever you're promoting "the setting" for the story (p. 255).
- Place CTAs in the signature of the article, not within the body. Give readers the option of clicking elsewhere without feeling "sold to" (p. 257).

## Language Rules

As a rule of thumb: readers on the internet don't like complicated (p. 238). "I'm advocating for making the deliberate choice to make your writing sound more natural" (p. 239).

- Write for the everyday person.
- Write how you speak. Record yourself talking about a topic, then transcribe it. Everyone who tries to sound professional ends up sounding inauthentic.
- Avoid long sentences.
- Alternate sentence length. Short sentences should be followed by longer sentences. Longer sentences should be followed by short sentences.
- Write confidently and declaratively.
- Replace adverbs with specifics. Don't tell the reader *how* something happened — show them. "She looked at him longingly" is vague; "She looked at him, unblinking, unable to turn away" is vivid. The adverb is a shortcut that skips the image. Unpack what the adverb *means* — the gesture, the detail, the consequence — and the writing becomes concrete and compelling. Common offenders: commonly, always, often, mostly, very, really, just, actually, truly. When you spot one, ask what it's hiding — then write that instead.

## Diagnosing Problems In Your Draft

If something feels off, check these common issues:

**The intro feels slow.** You're probably spending too many sentences before the promise. Try cutting to a 1/3/1. If you're using a 1/5/1, ask whether those extra two sentences are truly earning their place — or just delaying the point.

**Main Points all blur together.** They likely use the same framework and rhythm throughout. Mix structures: follow a deep 1/2/5/3/1 point with a fast 1/3/1 point. Use the Short/Long/Long/Short principle not just for paragraphs but across sections. Try bolding the first half of each opening sentence so the reader can see distinct topics at a glance.

**The piece feels long but says little.** Your Rate of Revelation is too low. Every sentence that doesn't advance the story should be cut. Check whether your main points overlap — if two points make the same argument in different words, merge them.

**You keep talking about yourself.** Check the ratio: your story or context should never equal or exceed the space you spend on the reader (p. 253). Reframe personal anecdotes so they serve as "the setting" for the reader's takeaway, not the main event.

**The conclusion feels forced.** You might not need one. Try the Extended Final Main Point — just add a sentence to your last point and stop. If the piece already made its argument, a formal conclusion can dilute the impact.

**The headline and intro don't match.** The headline sets a promise. The intro must deliver on it. If you wrote the intro first and the headline after, check whether the headline overpromises or promises something different from what the intro actually sets up. "Anytime you fail to deliver on your promise to a reader, you've lost them" (p. 149).

**Every sentence sounds the same.** You're not alternating sentence length. Short follows long. Long follows short. Read your draft aloud — monotone rhythm is easier to hear than to see.
