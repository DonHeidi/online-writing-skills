## Core Principle: Specificity Is The Secret

Before generating ideas, internalise this: the broader you are, the more confusing you are (p. 64). You want people to know where to put you on the bookshelf in their mind (p. 66). The more specific you are, the more clarity you gain as a writer — and the reader feels like they've found exactly who they were looking for (p. 67).

Start with a niche, then expand from there (p. 68). Every idea should pass the specificity test: "Can I make this more specific? Am I trying to cram too much into one article?" (p. 148).

## The Endless Idea Generator

A three-step system for generating ideas on demand (p. 266 ff).

### Step 1 — What "Type" Of Writing Is This?

Every piece leans toward one of five types. Knowing the type before you write shapes what kind of idea you need:

- **Actionable Guide** — Comprehensive, structured how-to content. Needs room for explanation and steps.
- **Opinion** — A clear stance backed by quotes, stories, and clarity. Needs a strong opening position.
- **Curated List** — A collection of examples or resources. Speed and specificity are everything.
- **Story** — A narrative built around conflict or achievement. Needs an emotional hook.
- **Credible Talking Head** — Authority-driven writing. Needs context for why your perspective is different.

### Step 2 — What "Idea" Am I Communicating?

The idea is the angle — the specific lens through which you approach a topic. Seven idea types:

- **Explanation** — Break down how something works or why it matters.
- **Habits** — What people do repeatedly that leads to success or failure.
- **Mistakes** — What people get wrong and how to fix it.
- **Lessons** — What you or others learned from experience.
- **Tips** — Tactical, actionable advice.
- **Stories** — Personal or observed narratives that illustrate a point.
- **Timely Events** — Something that just happened, applied to your niche.

### Step 3 — Why Me?

Your credibility angle — why the reader should listen to you on this topic:

- "I am an expert on this topic. Here is what I think."
- "I went out and talked to all the trusted experts on this topic. Here are their insights and opinions in one place."
- "I'm just sharing my opinion, but my opinion is the most articulate one of all."

### How It Works Together

Combine one writing type + one idea type + one credibility angle = one specific post idea. The same topic can generate dozens of distinct ideas by varying these three dimensions.

## Content Buckets

Before ideating individual posts, define your three content buckets (p. 263):

1. **General Audience** — Universal topics anyone can relate to. Life lessons, productivity, mindset. These have the broadest reach and highest viral potential (p. 14).
2. **Niche Audience** — Hyper-relevant to your expertise. The more specific, the better — refine "Sales" into "Cold Email Outreach For Sales" (p. 284).
3. **Company/Industry Audience** — Relevant to a specific industry or professional community.

The first six months should be spent exploring, practicing, and gathering data across all three buckets (p. 263). Use engagement data to discover which bucket and which specific topics within each bucket resonate most.

## Content Roadmap Template

A systematic way to go from buckets to a library of ideas (p. 269 ff):

**Step 1:** List your 3 Content Buckets (e.g. General: productivity, Niche: project management software, Industry: SaaS).

**Step 2:** List 3+ Topics under each bucket. These are the recurring themes you'll write about.

**Step 3:** Plug and Play — for each topic, run it through the Endless Idea Generator. Combine it with different writing types and idea types to produce distinct post concepts.

The goal: establishing yourself as an influential voice means writing at-length about the same handful of topics, and continuing to find new ways to approach them (p. 280). It's the same "story," but told hundreds of different ways (p. 281). Repetition is how you get people to remember you (p. 281).

## Differentiation: Making Each Idea "Better"

Once you have an idea, sharpen it by asking what makes it *better* than what already exists. The differentiation strategies depend on your writing type:

**Actionable Guide** — Better voice (more professional, more insightful), better organisation (superior structure), better positioned (a different perspective — e.g. instead of "how to charge your first client," write "why you shouldn't charge your first client"), better for the audience (broader or more specific), better experience (p. 150–151).

**Opinion** — Better quotes (insightful quotes from relevant sources), better stories (reframe the conversation by telling a unique story), better clarity (the clearest articulation wins) (p. 152–153).

**Curated List** — Better examples (lesser known, unconventional examples from seemingly irrelevant categories), better structure (incredibly fast Rate of Revelation), better subheads (each subhead is a sentence/thought in itself), better introduction (frame your list differently) (p. 154–157).

**Story** — Better openers (start at the moment of conflict or achievement), better characters (search for the unconventional) (p. 158–159).

**Credible Talking Head** — Better associations (name-dropping for credibility), better context (why your advice is different), better arguments (talk about what most people never learn), better perceptions (present yourself as "unlike the rest") (p. 161–163).

## Data-Driven Ideation

Writing online is a data mining machine (p. 44). Use data at every stage of ideation:

**Listen to what people want.** People tell you what they want — all you have to do is listen (p. 46). Double-down on topics that attract more viewership (p. 46). The questions people ask reveal their needs.

**Gather data early.** Start gathering data about what your most popular categories are (p. 123). Data doesn't lie, but data is also a reflection of the external crowd, not necessarily your internal compass (p. 124). Optimise for what performs so you can introduce people to what you care about (p. 125).

**Study the competition.** Pay close attention to writers at the top of the hierarchy and measure yourself against their performance (p. 125). Study why they are succeeding (p. 126). Question everything — nothing a creator does repeatedly is accidental (p. 127).

**Use alerts and trends.** Set alerts for companies and public figures that align with your topics, and wait for them to be mentioned in the news (p. 133). Timely events are idea triggers.

## Timely vs Timeless

Every idea falls somewhere on this spectrum (p. 260–262):

**Timely** — Responds to current events, trends, or news. Gets immediate attention but has a short shelf life. Only writing timely material makes it difficult to build a library of content that lasts (p. 260).

**Timeless** — Addresses enduring problems, principles, or questions. Builds a library of content that keeps working. The value of a library comes from the fact that it's timeless (p. 262). The more you write, the bigger your library of usable content grows (p. 262).

The strongest approach is a mix: use timely hooks to draw readers in, but anchor the piece in timeless principles so it stays useful.

## Niche Topics × Universal Questions

The best of both worlds: use niche topics to answer universal questions (p. 171). This lets you be specific enough to signal expertise while reaching a broader audience. A post about "cold email outreach" (niche) that answers "how to overcome fear of rejection" (universal) serves both your niche readers and a wider audience.

## Category Creation

When you've exhausted existing niches, create your own (p. 79). Three combination methods:

- **Audience × Genre** — e.g. "productivity for game developers" (p. 82)
- **Genre × Genre** — e.g. "stoicism meets startup culture" (p. 82)
- **Audience/Genre × Tone** — e.g. "finance explained with humor" (p. 83)

Your Point of View is the way you see the world, the unique vantage point that allows everyone else to understand your perspective (p. 83).

## Expanding Proven Ideas

Once a topic proves itself through data, expand it (p. 283 ff):

1. **Double down** — Write more about the same topic to confirm it wasn't a fluke (p. 283).
2. **Refine the bucket** — Narrow your content bucket to the specific angle that worked (p. 284).
3. **Create a Pillar Piece** — The most valuable, comprehensive, most engaging version of what's proven elsewhere (p. 285). Five ways to expand: combine content, curate expert opinions, add statistics, tell personal stories, provide more examples (p. 286).
4. **Build the flywheel** — Keep writing about the proven topic socially and link back to the Pillar Piece (p. 288).
5. **Go deeper** — Turn pillar pieces into downloadable guides, email courses, or products (p. 291).

## Quick-Reference: Idea Sparks

When stuck, run through these prompts:

- What question do I keep getting asked about [topic]?
- What's the biggest mistake people make with [topic]?
- What did I learn about [topic] that surprised me?
- What's the unconventional take on [topic] that most people get wrong?
- What's a story from my experience that illustrates [topic]?
- What just happened in the world that connects to [topic]?
- What would a curated list of the best [topic] resources look like?
- How can I use a niche example to answer a universal question?
- What would "the single best possible version" of a post about [topic] look like?
- What existing post of mine performed well that I can approach from a new angle?
