## Why Purpose Comes First

Without a purpose, you write about whatever is interesting in the moment. You connect with anyone. You rarely post. You get no results. Purpose is the prerequisite that makes every other decision — profile, audience, content, monetization — coherent (*60 Days to LinkedIn Mastery*, pp. 25–26).

When you have a purpose, you know who you want to reach and who you don't. You know what content to create. You know how to optimise your presence. Purpose is the filter that turns random activity into directed strategy.

This applies beyond any single platform. Whether writing on LinkedIn, Medium, Substack, or a personal blog, the underlying question is the same: **why are you writing online, and what do you want it to do for you?**

## The Two Writer Archetypes

The book identifies two fundamental starting positions (Ch. 1, p. 37):

**The Thought Leader** — Someone who wants to share insights, perspectives, and expertise. Their purpose is to build authority, influence how people think, and become a recognised voice in their category. Writing is the end in itself.

**The Solopreneur** — Someone who uses writing as a marketing mechanism for their business. Their purpose is to attract customers, demonstrate expertise, and drive business outcomes. Writing is the means to a business end.

Most writers are a blend of both, but knowing which one dominates shapes every downstream choice — what you write about, how you position yourself, and what success looks like.

## The Six Dimensions of Purpose

Purpose isn't a single statement — it's an intersection of several choices. These six dimensions, drawn from across the chapters, define a writer's purpose:

### 1. Motivation — Why are you writing online?

The fundamental driver. Common motivations include:

- Career change or advancement
- Building authority in a field
- Growing a business or finding customers
- Finding a job or attracting opportunities
- Sharing knowledge and teaching
- Processing ideas and thinking in public
- Building a personal brand
- Creative expression and storytelling

A clear motivation answers: "I'm writing online because ___." If you can't finish that sentence, your purpose is unclear (*60 Days to LinkedIn Mastery*, p. 26).

### 2. Audience — Who do you want to reach?

Purpose tells you who matters and who doesn't. Three audience layers (Ch. 10, p. 263):

- **General Audience** — Universal topics with the broadest reach
- **Niche Audience** — Hyper-relevant to your expertise
- **Company/Industry Audience** — Specific to a professional community

The sharper your purpose, the clearer your audience becomes. An executive making a career change writes for hiring managers and industry leaders. An entrepreneur growing a SaaS business writes for potential customers and partners.

### 3. Category — What do you want to be known for?

You want people to know where to put you on the bookshelf in their mind (Ch. 3, p. 66). Category is the answer to: "This person writes about ___."

Category can be chosen or created (Ch. 3, p. 79):

- **Chosen** — You pick an existing niche and aim to become the best voice in it
- **Created** — You combine dimensions to make a new category:
  - Audience × Genre (e.g. "productivity for game developers")
  - Genre × Genre (e.g. "stoicism meets startup culture")
  - Audience/Genre × Tone (e.g. "finance explained with humor")

Start with a niche, then expand from there (Ch. 3, p. 68).

### 4. Point of View — What's your unique perspective?

Your POV is the way you see the world, the unique vantage point that allows everyone else to understand your perspective (Ch. 3, p. 83). Two writers can occupy the same category but have completely different purposes because their POV differs.

POV emerges from the intersection of your experience, your beliefs, and your audience. It's not something you manufacture — it's something you discover through writing and paying attention to what resonates.

### 5. Style — Where do you sit on the spectrum?

Every writer sits somewhere on the Educating ↔ Entertaining spectrum (Ch. 3, p. 59). This isn't a permanent position — it shifts per piece — but your default tendency is part of your purpose.

The recommendation: don't overthink this. Get started writing, publishing, gathering data, "practice in public" (Ch. 3, p. 59). Your style will emerge from the doing, not from planning.

### 6. Vision — What does success look like beyond metrics?

Metrics (views, likes, followers) are feedback signals, not the destination. The deeper question is: what impact do you want your writing to have?

- "We don't need to be heard by millions of people in order to make an impact" (Ch. 12, p. 314)
- "The best path to making a living doing what you love is finding ways to monetize not necessarily the craft itself" (Ch. 12, p. 309)
- "What drives their purchase is what they believe your words can do for them" (Ch. 12, p. 316)

Vision is the answer to: "If my writing succeeds, what changes in the world — or in my life?"

## The Tension: Purpose vs. Data

Data tells you what the crowd wants. Purpose tells you what you care about. These often pull in different directions (Ch. 5, p. 124–125):

- "Data doesn't lie. But data is also a reflection of the external crowd, and not necessarily your internal compass."
- The resolution: "optimize for the former so you can introduce people to the latter."

This means: write what performs to build an audience, then use that audience's attention to share what matters to you. The 80/20 principle applies — most of your content serves the audience's interests, and a portion serves your deeper purpose.

A clear purpose makes this tension productive rather than paralyzing. You know which compromises are strategic and which ones are selling out.

## Purpose as Decision Filter

Once defined, purpose simplifies every subsequent choice:

- **What to write about?** → Does this topic serve my purpose?
- **Who to engage with?** → Does this person belong to my target audience?
- **Which platform?** → Does this platform reach the people I need to reach?
- **How to position myself?** → Tell people who you are, what category your writing is in, and what makes you credible (Ch. 3, p. 70)
- **How to monetize?** → What business model aligns with my purpose? (Ch. 12)
- **When to say no?** → Does this opportunity move me toward or away from my purpose?

Without purpose, these questions have no answer. With purpose, they answer themselves.

## Signs Your Purpose Is Unclear

- You write about whatever interests you in the moment with no connecting thread
- You can't describe your audience in one sentence
- You connect with or follow everyone without criteria
- You rarely post because you don't know what to say
- Your content gets no engagement because it's too scattered to build recognition
- You can't answer "why are you writing online?" in a single sentence
- You measure success only in metrics (followers, likes) because you have no deeper benchmark

---

## Signs Your Purpose Is Clear

- You can finish the sentence "I write online because ___" without hesitating
- You know exactly who your ideal reader is
- You have a category you want to own
- You know where you sit on the educating/entertaining spectrum
- You can articulate your POV — what makes your perspective different
- You have a vision for what success looks like beyond numbers
- You can use your purpose to make decisions about what to write and what to skip
